Before the next heavy rain

Flood prevention & sump pumps in Indianapolis, IN.

A practical guide to sump pumps, backup power, discharge routing, drainage coordination, and flood-prevention plumbing for Indianapolis-area homeowners.

Start with the system

One pump,
many failure points.

A basement can take on water even when the pump itself is working. Before replacing equipment, look at where water enters, how it reaches the basin, whether the pump can cycle freely, and where the discharge ends up.

01 · Collection
Pit & basin

The basin needs to collect water without a blocked inlet, loose cover, or float that cannot move.

02 · Pumping
Pump & float

The pump must lift water reliably, and the float must start it before water reaches a damaging level.

03 · Sending
Check valve & pipe

A check valve helps keep discharged water from dropping back into the pit when the pump stops.

04 · Releasing
Discharge route

Water needs a lawful, practical route away from the foundation and away from places it can return.

01 Sump pump work

Install, replace,
test before trouble.

A pump that is noisy, cycling too often, failing to start, or running without lowering the water level deserves attention before the next storm. The right answer may be a repair, a replacement, a pit correction, or a discharge correction—not automatically a bigger pump.

A simple homeowner check

Watch the whole cycle.

When conditions are dry and it is safe to do so, add water to the pit slowly enough to see the float rise. Confirm that the pump starts, the water level drops, and water leaves from the discharge point. Do not handle a pump, cord, outlet, or panel if any electrical component is wet or compromised.

If the pump hums without moving water, trips power, runs continuously, or the pit rises toward overflow, stop testing and get help.

02 Power & discharge

Plan for the rain
and the outage.

The moment a sump system is needed most can coincide with a power interruption. Battery backup systems are one option worth discussing, but their fit, runtime, and installation requirements depend on the specific pump and home.

The route matters

Water should not come home again.

A discharge that ends too close to the house, leaks beside the foundation, freezes shut, or aims toward a low spot can undermine a good pump. The route also needs to respect property lines and local requirements.

If runoff is pooling near the house, the solution may involve grading, extensions, downspout routing, or drainage work outside the pit. A plumbing visit can help identify which questions belong to the plumber and which belong to a drainage, foundation, or landscape professional.

Prevention around the home

A dry basement starts outside the basement.

01

Gutters & downspouts

Keep gutters clear, repair obvious leaks, and make sure downspouts carry roof water away instead of dumping it at the foundation.

02

Grade & surface flow

Observe where water gathers after a hard rain. Low areas beside the house, settled soil, and failed splash blocks can send surface water toward the structure.

03

Basement plumbing

For homes vulnerable to sewer backups, ask about whether a backwater valve is appropriate. It is not a universal add-on: the piping layout and local code matter.

04

Routine preparation

Keep the sump-pit area accessible. Know where the electrical panel and main water shutoff are, and avoid storing valuables directly on a basement floor.

Make a useful service call

The details that help a plumber diagnose faster.

I.

What changed?

Was this the first time? Did the pump run constantly, stop running, or lose power? Did water arrive after rain, a sewer backup, or a plumbing leak?

II.

Where is the water?

Note whether it is in the pit, along a wall, near a floor drain, around a fixture, or coming from overhead plumbing.

III.

Where does it go?

If safe, identify the discharge outlet and whether water is visible there when the pump runs. A photo from a dry, safe location can help.

IV.

What has been tried?

Share recent repairs, previous flooding, unusual sounds, breaker trips, or changes to landscaping, gutters, and downspouts.

Safety first

When it is more than a plumbing call.

A plumber can help with a sump system and many home plumbing conditions. Electrical hazards, fast-rising water, gas odors, and threats to people need a different first response.

What if water is close to outlets, a panel, or electrical equipment?
Do not enter standing water or touch electrical equipment when a shock hazard may exist. If it is safe to do so from a dry location, keep people away from the area. Contact the appropriate emergency or utility resource before any plumbing work is attempted.
What if I smell gas?
Leave the area, avoid switches, flames, and anything that could ignite gas, and contact the gas utility or emergency services from a safe location. Do not treat a gas odor as a sump-pump issue.
Can I use a portable pump or shop vacuum?
Only when the setup is appropriate and there is no electrical hazard. Do not use ordinary household equipment in unsafe standing water, and do not rely on it as a substitute for professional emergency response when water is rising or affecting utilities. For an already-flooded basement, follow official safety guidance; pumping out a flooded basement too quickly can create structural risk.
Should I add a backwater valve?
A backwater valve can be relevant for some homes with sewer-backup risk, but it must be chosen for the actual pipe layout and installed in a way that remains accessible for service. Ask for a site-specific evaluation rather than treating it as a standard fix.

For household flood safety and recovery information, see the Indiana Department of Homeland Security flood-safety guidance and FEMA’s homeowner flood guide.
